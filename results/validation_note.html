Method note · xT model comparison

A running test: forecast each matchweek before it is played

A proposal to put the xT and xG models into production against live fixtures. Every model in a published register forecasts each team’s npxG and goals before kick-off, from that team’s last six matches, and is scored as the results come in. What that buys over a backtest, how to run it, and how long before it tells us anything.

Why a running test and not another backtest

Action-valuation models have no ground truth. Karun’s xT, Soccermatics xT and VAEP each put a number on a pass, but no pass has a true value to check it against, and each model is fitted towards its own proxy — reaching a shot, scoring within ten actions. Ranking them on that proxy only asks which fits its own definition best.

Forecasting a later match fixes the circularity, and a backtest can do it. What a backtest cannot do is prove it did it honestly. Look-ahead is easy to introduce and hard to see. A grid of models, windows and targets will manufacture a winner that nobody named in advance. A forecast time-stamped before kick-off has neither problem: the match has not been played, so there is nothing to leak, and the entry was registered, so there is nothing to select.

The test

Before each matchweek, every entry in the register emits, for both teams in every fixture, a forecast of that team’s non-penalty xG and of its goals. The inputs are the team’s own action value created and its opponent’s conceded, each averaged over the previous six matches. The valuer is fitted on earlier seasons and other leagues; the forecasting regression is fitted on every match completed to date. Results arrive, forecasts are scored by RMSE, and the running tally updates.

Why six

Six is where the retrospective run points, and a demanding place to be judged. Averaging more matches lowers everyone’s error, but it also lets past npxG catch up and overtake: beyond about ten, no xT model separates from it. If an action valuation carries information that a simple tally of chances does not, a short history is where it has to show. Six is also the window a club actually has in hand — early in a season, or after a run of signings.

What goes in the register

Everything on one scale, so the comparison is like for like:

  • the xT and VAEP models under test, each as attack plus opponent defence;
  • two yardsticks that cost nothing — the team’s own past npxG, and its past goals;
  • the no-skill floor: always predict the league average.

Both targets are registered and neither may be chosen afterwards: in the retrospective run the ordering of the entries reverses between npxG and goals. A valuation that cannot beat the free yardsticks over a season has not earned its machinery.

Running it honestly

  1. Lock it before the season. The model specs, the six-match window, both targets, the action set and the code version, hashed and published — so a later change is visible as a change.
  2. One forecast per team per match, emitted before kick-off into an append-only log with its timestamp. A forecast that misses the deadline is a miss, not a late entry.
  3. The valuer refits on a fixed schedule only — between seasons — and never on the league it is currently rating.
  4. The regression refits after each completed matchweek, on everything up to it and nothing after.
  5. Score on arrival. Running RMSE for every entry, with team-clustered intervals on each difference, never one pooled number without them.
  6. A model added mid-stream is a new entrant with its own start date, compared only on the overlap.
  7. Publish the whole register, including entries that stop. A model that quietly disappears should be visible as having disappeared.

How long before it says anything

Set expectations from what the retrospective run needed. The differences between good models are 0.01–0.02 of RMSE, and separating them at 95 per cent took on the order of 2,300 scored team-matches — which is what a six-match window over four big leagues yields in a single season, once every team has played its first six and the regression has its minimum training set. So: one season for effects the size of the ones already seen, and nearer three for the smaller ones. A running test is a multi-season commitment, which is the argument for starting it now rather than after the next analysis.

What it still cannot tell us

The ceiling does not move: about a tenth of a single team-match is predictable, so no entry will look impressive in absolute terms and the floor belongs on every chart. The test scores a team-level aggregate, so it ranks ways of summing actions as much as ways of valuing them. And it answers “does this measure something that persists?” — not “is this the right value for this pass?”

What the retrospective run shows at six matches — and the claim the running test registers

At n = 6, on 2,314 team-matches

Soccermatics xT + npxG leads both targets: RMSE 0.650 against next-match npxG (R² 0.102, floor 0.686) and 1.139 against next-match goals (R² 0.070, floor 1.181). Karun and VAEP sit behind it on both, and past npxG alone is seventh of eight on the goals target.

The claim being registered

At six matches it is the only entry that beats both free yardsticks on both targets. Against past npxG: −0.0090 (p = 0.040) and −0.0146 (p = 0.046). Against past goals: −0.0147 (p = 0.005) and −0.0170 (p = 0.046). Four signs to confirm or break prospectively.

And two warnings

Six was read off this run, which is precisely why it must now be locked and tested forward. And an npxG × xT interaction looks strongly significant in sample (F up to 11) yet does not pay out of sample — it helps only where the regression has hundreds of rows to fit it. Scored live, week by week, that would have shown as a loss from the start.
