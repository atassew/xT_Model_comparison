Methods

Does past xT predict next-match npxG?

One common test for three action-valuation models: predict a team’s non-penalty xG in its next match from what it created, and what its opponent conceded, before it.

01

Data

StatsBomb open data, 2015/16. 1,551 matches, 3.0M actions. England, Spain, Italy and France are forecast; Germany is training-only.

02

Three valuations

Karun grid xT, Soccermatics xT and VAEP — all scored on the same actions, passes and crosses only, so none gets credit the others cannot.

03

Leave one league out

Each method is fitted on the other leagues, then values the league held out. No model ever sees the league it rates.

04

Features

Own value created plus the opponent’s value conceded, each averaged over the previous n matches, n = 1 to 30. Never its own match.

05

Forecast

Walk-forward least squares per league, refitted on every match played before that date and nothing after it. Target: next-match npxG.

06

Scoring

Root mean squared error, the loss the fit itself minimises. Tested by team-block bootstrap and Diebold–Mariano, clustered on 80 teams.

Results

Past npxG is the baseline to beat

378 team-matches, one common scored set. Colour = xT method; solid = xT pair, dotted = all four terms, heavy blue dashes = npxG only, no xT.

npxG alone is hard to beat

Its best error is RMSE 0.675 at n = 27, the lowest of any model at any window.

npxG always helps an xT model

Adding it to any of the three moves RMSE by −0.017 to −0.027, significant at every window.

xT helps only on short histories

Soccermatics + npxG beats npxG alone at n = 5 (p = 0.037) and n = 7 (p = 0.011). By n = 10 the gap is gone.

More matches stop paying

The npxG curve is flat from about n = 24: a longer history buys nothing more.

Predict goals instead, and xT comes out ahead

Target is non-penalty goals — penalties and own goals excluded, exactly as in npxG. Figure: 378 team-matches on the common scored set. The tests at right use n = 7 on its own full set of 2,232 forecasts.

Goals are far harder to call

The best model reaches R² ≈ 0.09, against 0.12 for npxG. Most of a single match stays unpredictable.

Past goals is the weakest signal

All three xT models beat it: Soccermatics −0.016 (p = 0.019), Karun −0.014 (p = 0.017), VAEP −0.018 (p = 0.001).

Past npxG does not beat it

−0.008, p = 0.124. On one season, xG is no better than the scoreline at forecasting the scoreline.

Soccermatics + npxG wins again

RMSE 1.140 at n = 7, ahead of npxG alone (p = 0.044) — the same model that led on the npxG target.

Conclusions · judged on goals

Judged on goals, xT beats the tallies — xG does not

Goals are the honest target, and a brutal one. From six matches of history only about 7 per cent of a team’s next-match goals is predictable: RMSE 1.139 against a no-model floor of 1.181.

Past goals is the weakest predictor of goals, last of eight. At seven matches all three xT models beat it (p = 0.001–0.019) while past npxG does not (p = 0.124).

Soccermatics xT + npxG leads. At six matches it alone beats both free yardsticks (p = 0.046 on each).

The npxG verdict reverses. Forecasting npxG, past npxG wins and xT adds almost nothing; forecasting goals, xT is what separates. The target decides the answer, so it must be fixed in advance.

Two things that do not pay. An npxG × xT interaction is strongly significant in sample but worse out of sample when training rows are short; best action per sequence helps VAEP alone.

Next: lock six matches and both targets in a register, and score it forward.

Caveats: one season only; the best model beats the best yardstick by about one and a half per cent of forecast error; and the winners were picked out of a grid of eight models, thirty windows and two targets — which is exactly why six matches and both targets now get registered in advance.
